# The First-Account Checklist

For the day your kid is old enough for their own AI account — typically somewhere between 13 and 16, depending on your family, your terms of service laws, and your kid. Walk the checklist together, in one sitting, before the account gets used.

## Before the account exists

- [ ] **Check the tool's actual minimum age.** Most general chat assistants require 13 (with parent permission), and some require 18 without it. Ignore the "just lie about the birth year" shortcut; you're teaching honesty, and age flags also change which protections the tool applies.
- [ ] **Pick the right plan.** Free tier first. Consider the family/teen-plan options if the tool offers one — some (like "teen mode" or family plans) add guardrails, stricter filters, and parent-visible settings.
- [ ] **Read the privacy options once.** Find the settings that: turn off chat-history training / sharing, disable personalized ads, limit memory. You don't need a law degree — just find and toggle.
- [ ] **Set the account email to a managed one.** A family email or your address first — so password resets and warnings land in front of you, not in a parallel universe.
- [ ] **Create the password with them.** Password manager or a written card at home; never reused; never shared — *with anyone*, including "my best friend" and especially with "my AI."

## The account's first day

- [ ] **Walk the settings tour together:** history, memory, personalization, exports, and the report/flag button. Find them once so they're findable in a bad moment.
- [ ] **Name it honestly.** Their real first name, no birth-year-2172 or "totally-not-13" usernames — accounts get school-attached and reputation-attached.
- [ ] **First test drive:** do a task you do together — "explain this math concept" — then check the answer. Their first AI result should be verified, on purpose, so the habit starts day one.
- [ ] **Set the first prompt example:** "Explain X to a 14-year-old" is a fine default style.

## The standing rules (goes on the [family agreement sheet](family-agreement-sheet.html))

- [ ] **Sharing rules still apply** — no photos of faces, no last name, no home location, no big feelings ([Sharing Rules](04-sharing-rules-photos-names-feelings.html)).
- [ ] **Their own account doesn't change homework rules** — [School Policies](02-homework-honesty-and-school-policies.html) travel with them to every tool.
- [ ] **The tell-me rule is stronger now** — screenshots always welcome; trouble confessed is trouble halved ([Bad AI Interaction](05-spotting-a-bad-ai-interaction.html)).
- [ ] **No account changes alone** — no new email on the account, no switching to a different premium tool, no un-deleting history. Changes need a parent again.
- [ ] **Friend-bot warnings still apply** — and matter *more* now that it's their personal account ([When a Chatbot Feels Like a Friend](03-when-a-chatbot-feels-like-a-friend.html)).

## The 30-day check-in

One month after the account starts, hold the review:

- What have they used it for? (Actually ask; be curious, not interrogating.)
- Anything surprising, weird, or uncomfortable in the history?
- Which settings we turned off are still off?
- What should next month's rule be — any earned independence to grant?

Then set the next review (same time next month), and keep the first one's tone: curiosity over surveillance.

## Signs to slow it back down

- Grades drop while AI use climbs
- Sleep slips — late-night chats
- Mood dips after sessions, or they hide the screen fast
- Their bot starts showing up in their language, jokes, or worldview
- Any of the red flags on [Spotting a Bad AI Interaction](05-spotting-a-bad-ai-interaction.html)

If any of these show up: pause the account, keep the relationship, and talk before you restrict. Restrictions without conversation get bypassed; conversation with restriction sticks.

## One-line takeaway

**An account is a grant of independence — hand it over with a checklist, keep the settings visible, and review it at month one and every month after that.**
