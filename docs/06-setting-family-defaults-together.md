# Setting Family Defaults Together

One short evening at the kitchen table. By the end, your family will have a one-page set of AI defaults that everyone — including you — can follow. Kids follow rules they helped write far better than rules handed down.

## Why "together" is the whole trick

Unilateral rules get outsmarted by a kid with a tablet and eleven spare minutes. Co-authored rules get defended *by* the kid — because "we agreed" beats "because I said so." You're not giving them power; you're giving them authorship, which earns you compliance.

## The meeting, in five steps (~30 minutes, snacks help)

1. **Set the scene (5 min).** "We're making our family AI rules together. Nothing is decided yet; we decide it as a family."
2. **Names the tools (5 min).** List which AI tools your family actually uses: the chat assistant, the image generator, the homework helper, voice assistants, that one game. "AI" is too abstract; your actual tools are concrete.
3. **Tell them what AI is (5 min).** Use the guide: [What AI Actually Is](01-what-ai-actually-is.html). Assuming tool-level fluency helps every later rule.
4. **Draft defaults together (10 min).** Use the pull-down menu below; let kids pick the wording for a couple rules. Kids will pick more than you'd expect; resist the urge to over-tighten.
5. **Sign the sheet (5 min).** Print [the family agreement sheet](family-agreement-sheet.html), fill it with actual names, have everyone sign, and put it near where the tools get used.

## The seven family defaults to set

Work through these one at a time. Kid picks the words, parent holds the line.

1. **Who can use which tools — and which device** ("homework tablet only," "with headphones but not in your room right now")?
2. **When:** homework-and-learning first? Nothing after what time? Balanced on weekends?
3. **What's off-limits:** the sharing rules — no faces, no names, no big feelings ([Photos, Names, Feelings](04-sharing-rules-photos-names-feelings.html)).
4. **Homework honesty:** the school policy plus the three honest uses ([Homework Honesty](02-homework-honesty-and-school-policies.html)).
5. **The tell-me rule:** show a parent any scary, weird, or stuck-big chat — and *no one gets in trouble for telling*.
6. **Talking to it like a friend:** any bot that starts acting like a person with feelings is a stop-and-tell ([Bad AI Interaction](05-spotting-a-bad-ai-interaction.html)).
7. **The review date:** rules get revisited at a fixed date (kids' birthday works). Not renegotiated weekly; not frozen forever.

## Two defaults worth naming out loud

- **Adults follow these rules too.** A parent who narrates their AI use ("I'm asking it to help draft this email — let me check what it wrote") teaches more than any rule page.
- **The rules bend with age, not with arguing.** More independence comes from the age band below, not from negotiation fatigue.

## When someone breaks a default

Consequence, conversation, and the agreement stays. The tell-me rule exists to be used in failure moments; a kid who confesses gets the softer path, always. That asymmetry is what makes your information flow work for the next five years.

## One-line takeaway

**Defaults written at your kitchen table, signed by everyone, and revisited once a year will outperform every parental-control app combined.**
