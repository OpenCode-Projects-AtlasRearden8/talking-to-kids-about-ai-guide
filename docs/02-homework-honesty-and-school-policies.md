# Homework Honesty & School Policies

The question isn't whether your kid *can* use AI for homework. It's whether they can use it the way their teacher actually allows — and still learn the thing the homework exists to teach.

## Start here: find your school's actual policy

Before any conversation, check for one of these:

- the student handbook (search it for "AI", "artificial intelligence", or "ChatGPT")
- a syllabus or class page — many teachers now state their own AI rules per assignment
- the district website — some publish K-12 AI guidance

If you can't find anything, that's normal. Then the rule lives with each teacher, and *asking the teacher* becomes the skill to teach your kid. That's a genuinely useful life skill; frame it positively.

> "When you're not sure what's allowed, email the teacher and ask. 'Can I use an AI tool for this, and if so, what parts?' Teachers respect that question a lot more than they respect discovering you did the whole essay with AI."

## The three honest uses (a good family rule)

If your school has no clear policy, this is a solid default to adopt at home — it matches what many teachers mean in practice:

1. **Understand:** "Explain the American Revolution like I'm ten." That's like asking a librarian. Fine.
2. **Practice:** "Quiz me on my Spanish verbs." Fine.
3. **Review:** "Here's my finished essay — tell me where my argument is weak." Fine, *if* your kid still does the writing and it's allowed for that class.

## The three dishonest uses (the ones that backfire)

1. **Replacing:** paste the prompt, submit AI's essay. Your kid learns nothing and gets caught easily — teachers compare writing samples and know a voice that isn't your kid's.
2. **Fabricating:** "Write a list of quotes with sources," or asking AI for facts, statistics, or citations without checking them. AI invents citations constantly, and a fake citation is one of the fastest ways to get flagged.
3. **Hiding:** using AI when it's not allowed and not saying so. The cover-up — not the AI — is what turns small trouble into big trouble.

## The phrase that keeps everything honest

Teach your kid one sentence that works with every teacher:

> "I used AI to [help me understand / practice / review]. Here's how I used it."

Disclosed AI use is boring. Hidden AI use is a discipline matter. The *disclosure* is the whole game.

## Grading your kid's AI use, fast

Ask these three questions together; they fit on a fridge sticky note:

- **Did it help you learn the thing, or instead of you learning the thing?** Homework is a practice machine. Using AI so you skip the practice is like using a calculator to skip learning multiplication — it works until the day you can't.
- **Would your teacher be comfortable hearing exactly how you used it?** If the answer is no, it's already not okay.
- **Did you check it?** AI gets homework *wrong* regularly — wrong math steps, wrong dates, wrong citations. Submitting unverified AI work as your own is a two-for-one: cheating *and* wrong.

## When your kid got caught (or you suspect it)

Don't lead with the lecture. Ask the two questions teachers actually care about:

1. "How much of this is your own thinking?"
2. "What did you use AI for?"

Then align on the repair: talk to the teacher first (kid, not parent, usually), redo the work honestly, and accept whatever consequence is fair. A parent who defends the cover-up teaches the cover-up.

## If your school bans AI outright

Say so plainly: "At school, the rule is no AI. That's their classroom, so that's the rule there." Then note that the *home* rule can be different for learning-at-home purposes — practicing piano isn't the concert. Clear lines beat vague ones.

## One-line takeaway

**Honest AI use is disclosed, checked, and used to learn — and when the rules are unclear, you ask the teacher instead of guessing.**
